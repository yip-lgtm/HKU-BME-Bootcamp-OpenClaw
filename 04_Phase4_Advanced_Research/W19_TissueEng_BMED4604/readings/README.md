# Week 19 Readings — Tissue Engineering (BMED4604)

## Required Readings

### Primary Literature (Classic Papers)

1. **Langer, R. & Vacanti, J.P.** (1993) "Tissue Engineering" — *Science*, 260(5110), 920-926. DOI: 10.1126/science.8493529
   - The foundational paper that defined the field. Introduces the "triad" concept: cells, scaffolds, and signaling molecules.
   - Key insight: Tissue engineering is a "new approach to the replacement and repair of tissues and organs."

2. **Atala, A.** (2009) "Engineering Tissues, Organs and Cells" — *Methods in Enzymology*, 467, 3-28. DOI: 10.1016/S0076-6879(09)67002-7
   - Comprehensive review of cell sources, scaffold materials, and bioreactor design.
   - Discusses clinical translation of engineered tissues.

3. **Ratner, B.D., Hoffman, A.S., Schoen, F.J. & Lemons, J.E.** (2013) *Biomaterials Science: An Introduction to Materials in Medicine*, 3rd ed. — Elsevier. ISBN: 978-0123746269
   - Chapter 4: "Scaffolds for Tissue Engineering" (pp. 235-316)
   - Chapter 8: "Tissue Engineering" (pp. 597-658)

4. **Okano, T. & Matsuda, T.** (1997) "Hybrid muscular tissues: Preparation of skeletal muscle cell sheets from cell lines for tissue engineering" — *Artificial Organs*, 21(11), 1197-1202.
   - Cell sheet engineering — a Japanese approach to tissue assembly without scaffolds.

5. **Khademhosseini, A. & Langer, R.** (2007) "Microengineered hydrogels for tissue engineering" — *Biomaterials*, 28(32), 5087-5092. DOI: 10.1016/j.biomaterials.2007.07.021

### Key Papers (Research Depth)

1. **Ma, P.X. & Choi, J.W.** (2001) "Biodegradable polymer scaffolds with well-defined interconnected spherical pores" — *Advanced Functional Materials*, 11(1), 67-76.
   - Systematic study of scaffold pore architecture effects on tissue regeneration.

2. **Lutolf, M.P. & Hubbell, J.A.** (2005) "Synthetic biomaterials as instructive extracellular microenvironments for morphogenesis in tissue engineering" — *Nature Biotechnology*, 23(1), 47-55.
   - Hubbell's landmark contribution: synthetic ECM mimetics with precise signaling control.

3. **Griffith, A.L. et al.** (1989) "Meniscus regeneration in rabbits using a carbon fiber scaffold" — *Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research*, 244, 265-268.
   - Early tissue engineering of meniscus; the challenge remains largely unsolved.

4. **Vunjak-Novakovic, G. et al.** (2004) "Bioreactor cultivation of engineered cartilage" — *Advances in Experimental Medicine and Biology*, 559, 147-160.
   - Dynamic bioreactor culture to overcome diffusion limits in tissue engineering.

5. **Phelps, E.A. et al.** (2010) "Bioartificial matrices for therapeutic vascularization" — *PNAS*, 107(8), 3323-3328.
   - VEGF release kinetics from biomaterial matrices for vascular network formation.

## Learning Objectives (BMED4604)

By the end of Week 19, students should be able to:

1. Explain the "tissue engineering triad" — cells, scaffolds, signaling — and their interdependence
2. Compare decellularized organ scaffolds vs. synthetic polymer scaffolds, including their respective advantages and limitations
3. Describe stem cell trilineage differentiation (osteogenic, chondrogenic, adipogenic) and the molecular signals controlling each
4. Apply growth factor release kinetics models (zero-order, first-order, Higuchi) to design controlled release systems
5. Explain how mechanical stimulation in bioreactors (tensile, compressive, shear) affects tissue maturation
6. Identify the critical barriers to clinical translation in tissue engineering (vascularization, scale-up, immune response)
7. Critically evaluate literature on scaffold porosity, pore size, and degradation rate requirements for specific tissue types
