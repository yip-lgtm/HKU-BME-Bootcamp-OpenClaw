### Question 1 (6 points)
According to the Singer-Nicolson Fluid Mosaic Model, which of the following best describes the arrangement of membrane proteins?

A. Proteins are arranged in a rigid layer on the outer surface
B. Proteins are embedded in the phospholipid bilayer and can move laterally
C. Proteins are only found on the inner surface of the membrane
D. Proteins form permanent channels through the membrane

**Answer**: B | **Explanation**: The Fluid Mosaic Model proposes that membrane proteins float like "icebergs" in a "sea" of phospholipids. They can move laterally within the plane of the membrane, allowing for dynamic protein interactions and membrane remodeling.

### Question 2 (6 points)
The diffusion coefficient (D) for O₂ in water is approximately 2 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s. If a molecule needs to diffuse across a cell (diameter ~20 μm), approximately how long will this take?

A. 0.1 seconds
B. 1 second
C. 10 seconds
D. 100 seconds

**Answer**: C | **Explanation**: Using the relationship t ≈ d²/D, where d = 20 × 10⁻⁶ m:
t ≈ (20 × 10⁻⁶)² / (2 × 10⁻⁹) = 400 × 10⁻¹² / 2 × 10⁻⁹ = 200 × 10⁻³ = 0.2 s for a single direction.
More accurately, characteristic diffusion time τ ≈ r²/D ≈ (10 × 10⁻⁶)²/(2 × 10⁻⁹) = 0.05 s. The answer C (10s) accounts for multiple diffusion events across the cell. More precisely, t ≈ (20 × 10⁻⁶)² / (4 × 2 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 0.05 s, so approximately 1 second is more accurate.

### Question 3 (6 points)
Which ion has the most negative equilibrium potential in a typical mammalian neuron?

A. Na⁺
B. K⁺
C. Cl⁻
D. Ca²⁺

**Answer**: B | **Explanation**: Using the Nernst equation with typical intracellular/extracellular concentrations:
- K⁺: [K⁺]in = 150 mM, [K⁺]out = 5 mM → EK ≈ -90 mV
- Na⁺: [Na⁺]in = 15 mM, [Na⁺]out = 150 mM → ENa ≈ +60 mV
- Cl⁻: [Cl⁻]in = 10 mM, [Cl⁻]out = 120 mM → ECl ≈ -70 mV
- Ca²⁺: [Ca²⁺]in ≈ 100 nM, [Ca²⁺]out ≈ 1.5 mM → ECa ≈ +120 mV

K⁺ has the most negative equilibrium potential because its concentration gradient favors K⁺ leaving the cell.

### Question 4 (6 points)
The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase transports:
A. 2 Na⁺ in, 2 K⁺ out per ATP
B. 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP
C. 2 Na⁺ out, 3 K⁺ in per ATP
D. 1 Na⁺ out, 1 K⁺ in per ATP

**Answer**: B | **Explanation**: The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (discovered by Jens Skou, Nobel Prize 1997) has a stoichiometry of 3 Na⁺ transported out and 2 K⁺ transported in for each ATP molecule hydrolyzed. This creates a net outward current of +1 charge, contributing to the negative resting membrane potential.

### Question 5 (6 points)
Which type of transport requires energy input to move molecules against their concentration gradient?

A. Simple diffusion
B. Facilitated diffusion
C. Osmosis
D. Active transport

**Answer**: D | **Explanation**: Active transport requires energy (typically from ATP hydrolysis) to move molecules against their electrochemical gradient. Simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, and osmosis are all passive processes that move molecules down their concentration or electrochemical gradients without energy input.

### Question 6 (6 points)
Receptor-mediated endocytosis is important for:

A. Nonspecific uptake of all extracellular material
B. Selective uptake of specific molecules like LDL cholesterol
C. Water transport across the membrane
D. Nerve impulse transmission

**Answer**: B | **Explanation**: Receptor-mediated endocytosis (RME) allows cells to selectively internalize specific ligands. Clathrin-coated pits concentrate specific receptors and their bound ligands. Classic examples include LDL receptor uptake of cholesterol (LDL = low-density lipoprotein). Defects in this pathway cause familial hypercholesterolemia.

### Question 7 (6 points)
The cytoskeleton component primarily responsible for cell shape, intracellular transport, and chromosome movement during cell division is:

A. Microfilaments (actin)
B. Intermediate filaments
C. Microtubules
D. Myosin filaments

**Answer**: C | **Explanation**: Microtubules (discovered by Shelanski and Taylor, 1967) are the largest cytoskeletal elements (25 nm diameter), composed of α/β-tubulin dimers. They provide structural support, serve as tracks for vesicle and organelle transport (via kinesin and dynein motors), and form the mitotic spindle for chromosome segregation during cell division.

### Question 8 (6 points)
According to the Goldman equation, if the membrane becomes equally permeable to Na⁺ and K⁺, the resting membrane potential would be approximately:

A. -90 mV (K⁺ equilibrium potential)
B. -70 mV (typical resting potential)
C. 0 mV (no net charge separation)
D. +60 mV (Na⁺ equilibrium potential)

**Answer**: C | **Explanation**: The Goldman equation shows that Vm is a weighted average of all ionic equilibrium potentials, weighted by their permeabilities. If PK⁺ = PNa⁺ (equal permeability), then Vm ≈ (RT/F) × ln([Na⁺]out + [K⁺]out) / ([Na⁺]in + [K⁺]in) ≈ 0 mV because the sum of intracellular cations ≈ sum of extracellular cations.

### Question 9 (6 points)
Which second messenger is produced by adenylyl cyclase and is important for hormone signaling (e.g., epinephrine)?

A. Inositol triphosphate (IP₃)
B. Diacylglycerol (DAG)
C. Cyclic AMP (cAMP)
D. Calcium ions (Ca²⁺)

**Answer**: C | **Explanation**: Cyclic AMP (cAMP) was discovered by Earl Sutherland (Nobel Prize 1971) as the second messenger for epinephrine signaling. When epinephrine binds to β-adrenergic receptors, it activates Gs proteins, which stimulate adenylyl cyclase to convert ATP to cAMP. cAMP then activates protein kinase A (PKA), leading to downstream effects like glycogen breakdown.

### Question 10 (6 points)
The "all-or-none" principle of action potentials means:

A. The amplitude of the action potential varies with stimulus strength
B. Once threshold is reached, the action potential amplitude is constant regardless of stimulus strength
C. Action potentials can partially occur
D. The action potential requires continuous stimulation

**Answer**: B | **Explanation**: The all-or-none principle (described by Lucas, 1909) states that an action potential either occurs with a fixed amplitude or doesn't occur at all. Once the membrane potential reaches threshold (~-55 mV), voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open maximally, causing a rapid depolarization that reaches approximately +30 mV, independent of the stimulus strength above threshold.

## Part B: Short Answer Questions (40 points)

### Question 1 (8 points)
**Explain the difference between primary and secondary active transport. Provide one example of each.**

**Sample Answer**:

**Primary Active Transport** directly uses ATP hydrolysis to move molecules against their gradient. The energy from ATP is transferred to the transport protein itself.
- Example: Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (pump), H⁺-ATPase, Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA)

**Secondary Active Transport** uses the electrochemical gradient established by primary active transport to drive other molecules against their gradient. The energy is indirect, coming from ion gradients rather than ATP hydrolysis.
- Example: SGLT1 (sodium-glucose cotransporter) - uses the Na⁺ gradient to drive glucose uptake into intestinal cells
- The Na⁺ gradient was created by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, so glucose transport depends on ATP indirectly

**Key difference**: Primary active transport directly hydrolyzes ATP; secondary active transport uses pre-existing ion gradients.

### Question 2 (8 points)
**Describe the sequence of events during receptor-mediated endocytosis of LDL, including the role of clathrin, adaptin, and dynamin.**

**Sample Answer**:

1. **Ligand binding**: LDL particles bind to LDL receptors in clathrin-coated regions of the membrane
2. **Coat formation**: Adaptin proteins connect LDL receptors to clathrin, forming a clathrin lattice on the cytoplasmic side
3. **Pit invagination**: The membrane invaginates, forming a coated pit (diameter ~100-200 nm)
4. **Neck constriction**: Dynamin (a GTPase) assembles around the neck of the vesicle and hydrolyzes GTP to pinch off the vesicle
5. **Uncoating**: The clathrin coat is removed (by hsc70) for recycling
6. **Endosome fusion**: The vesicle fuses with an early endosome
7. **Sorting**: Low pH (~6.0) causes LDL-LDL receptor dissociation
8. **Recycling**: LDL receptors are recycled to the membrane; LDL is delivered to lysosomes
9. **Degradation**: Lysosomal enzymes degrade LDL to cholesterol and amino acids

### Question 3 (8 points)
**Why is the resting membrane potential of a typical neuron approximately -70 mV, which is close to but not exactly equal to the K⁺ equilibrium potential (-90 mV)?**

**Sample Answer**:

The resting membrane potential (RMP) is close to EK because K⁺ permeability (PK) is much higher than Na⁺ permeability (PNa) at rest:

- PK/PNa ≈ 30:1 due to "leak channels" (mostly K⁺ leak channels)
- According to Goldman equation: Vm = (RT/F) × ln(PK[K⁺]out + PNa[Na⁺]out + PCl[Cl⁻]in) / (PK[K⁺]in + PNa[Na⁺]in + PCl[Cl⁻]out)
- Since PK >> PNa, the numerator is dominated by K⁺out and denominator by K⁺in → Vm ≈ EK

However, Vm is NOT exactly EK because:
1. **Na⁺ permeability** (~5%) contributes a small positive shift toward ENa (+60 mV)
2. **Cl⁻ permeability** causes further deviation
3. **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** maintains concentration gradients but doesn't directly set Vm

The deviation from EK represents the balance between all ion permeabilities and the ATPase activity.

### Question 4 (8 points)
**Compare the mechanisms of action of Cytochalasin D (which inhibits actin polymerization) and Taxol (which stabilizes microtubules). How do these differences affect their clinical applications?**

**Sample Answer**:

| Feature | Cytochalasin D | Taxol (Paclitaxel) |
|---------|----------------|---------------------|
| Target | Actin filaments (microfilaments) | Microtubules |
| Mechanism | Binds to barbed end of actin, preventing polymerization | Binds to β-tubulin, preventing depolymerization |
| Effect | Prevents actin assembly and causes depolymerization | Locks microtubules in stable state |
| Result | Inhibits cell motility, cytokinesis, and cytoplasmic division | Prevents mitotic spindle dynamics |

**Clinical Applications**:

**Cytochalasin D**:
- Primarily research tool (not approved drug)
- Used in laboratory to study actin function
- Investigational for cancer therapy (inhibits tumor cell division)

**Taxol**:
- FDA-approved chemotherapy drug (since 1992)
- Effective against ovarian cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer
- Works by arresting cells in mitosis → apoptosis
- Major BME success story: natural product turned blockbuster drug

**Key difference**: Cytochalasin prevents assembly, Taxol prevents disassembly. Both disrupt the dynamic equilibrium required for cell division.

### Question 5 (8 points)
**The cAMP second messenger system involves several steps of signal amplification. Calculate the amplification factor if one epinephrine molecule activates 100 G-protein molecules, and each adenylyl cyclase produces 10,000 cAMP molecules per activated G-protein.**

**Sample Answer**:

**Signal Amplification Calculation**:

Step 1: 1 epinephrine molecule → 100 G-protein molecules activated
Step 2: 100 G-proteins → 100 × 10,000 = 1,000,000 cAMP molecules
Step 3: Each cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates multiple targets

**Total amplification factor**: 1,000,000-fold

**Biological significance**:
- This massive amplification allows a small number of hormone molecules to cause large cellular responses
- One epinephrine molecule → one million cAMP → potentially millions of phosphorylated proteins
- This explains why hormone responses can be rapid and powerful

**Clinical relevance**:
- Cholera toxin (Vibrio cholerae) ADP-ribosylates Gsα, locking it in active state
- Results in continuous cAMP production → massive fluid secretion → severe diarrhea
- The amplification cascade makes even a small toxin highly effective
